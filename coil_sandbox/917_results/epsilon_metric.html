How piecewise-planar is a coil, and does it matter?

A cheap, exact measure of how far a coil is from being made of B flat pieces — what it computes, the validation that it recovers known structure, and the measurement showing it ranks designs correctly while badly overstating what the restriction costs.

What it measures

Piecewise-planar coils are attractive because flat pieces are cheap to build. Given a coilset, we want a number for how far it is from that form — ideally one cheap enough to sweep across many equilibria, to ask which configurations admit simple coils at all.

Define the defect of a closed coil as the best achievable out-of-plane residual when the curve is cut into B contiguous arcs, each fitted by its own plane:

εB = √( minsegmentations Σseg wseg · λmin(seg) / W ) ÷ (in-plane extent) λmin(seg) = smallest eigenvalue of that segment's arclength-weighted covariance = its mean squared distance from its own best-fit plane. Normalising by the coil's own in-plane extent makes ε dimensionless and comparable across machines.

ε1 is "how planar"; ε2 is "how clamshell-able"; and the decay of εB with B is the real signature. A coil whose ε2 is already negligible suits a two-arc class; one still poor at ε4 does not belong to this family at all.

How it is computed — exactly, not searched

The apparent cost is a search over where to place B breakpoints. It isn't, for two reasons that compose:

  • Every candidate arc is O(1). A covariance over any contiguous range follows from prefix sums of w, w·x and w·xxᵀ, so the cost of all O(n²) candidate arcs is one pass plus a batch of 3×3 eigendecompositions.
  • The optimal segmentation is a dynamic program. Partitioning a sequence into B contiguous pieces at minimum total cost is the classic segmented-least-squares DP. The curve is closed, so fix one breakpoint, run the DP, and minimise over that choice.

The result is the exact optimum in O(n²B) per fixed start — milliseconds per coil at n = 180. The optimal breakpoints come out as a by-product, which is separately useful: they are the hinge placement that a projection onto the arc classes should use, rather than a guess. Unexpected result

ε₁ appears to be a property of the equilibrium, not of the coil family. Coil 0 reads 0.105 / 0.097 / 0.100 / 0.082 and coil 2 reads 0.290 / 0.291 / 0.291 / 0.293 across four independently optimised families — including an unrestricted 3D coilset that started from the planar optimum and was free to go anywhere.

The unconstrained optimiser does not use more out-of-plane content than a two-plane coil; it distributes the same amount differently. That is the property a cross-equilibrium sweep needs, and it also makes such a sweep cheap — a converged unconstrained coilset per equilibrium may not be required, since a restricted run reports the same ε₁.

It ranks correctly

Two planes are worse than three both geometrically (the ceiling's ε₂ = 0.029–0.080 exceeds its ε₃ = 0.023–0.031) and in field error (1.24× versus 1.20×). The ordering holds — on two points.

It badly overstates the cost

The decisive measurement

The unrestricted optimum sits at ε₂ ≈ 0.08 of its in-plane extent — a geometric distance of order 0.3 m from any two-plane coil, and a refit of a far milder case already missed by 0.63 m. Yet the best two-plane coilset costs only 1.24× the field error.

Worse, the two quantities move at different rates: from B = 2 to B = 3 the geometric defect falls 2.6× while the field-error penalty improves only 17% relative (1.24× → 1.20×).

The reason is structural. The right quantity is the gap between two constrained optima, which is not a distance in coil space at all. The objective is flat in precisely the directions the restriction removes, so a coilset can be metres away and barely worse.

In engineering terms the restriction is nearly free: at the measured length elasticity d(ln bn)/d(ln L) ≈ −2.1, a factor of 1.24 in field error is worth about 11% more conductor. Forcing every coil into two flat pieces costs roughly what shortening them from 36 m to 32 m would.

How to use it

  • As a screen and a ranking, never as a penalty estimate. It is ~10³ times cheaper than the real quantity, which needs two optimisation runs. That is its value; it has to earn its correlation rather than assume it.
  • As a hinge-placement generator. The DP's optimal breakpoints are a principled projection onto the arc classes, and distinct local minima of the segmentation landscape are candidate basins — a structured alternative to random or centroid-line hinge choices.
  • With an admissibility flag alongside it. εB is a lower bound on what a real class achieves. Polar arcs additionally require θ to advance monotonically about each chord midpoint; we found a coilset that was planar per segment to 1e−14 m and still outside the class, because a deep re-entrant corner made it overshoot θ = π.

A refinement worth building

εB weights every point equally, but a large out-of-plane excursion on the outboard side, far from the plasma, costs far less field error than a small one inboard. Weighting each point by its inverse distance to the boundary — or by the local B·n sensitivity — should correlate much better, at no extra cost.

Limits, and the result that reframes all of it

  • One equilibrium, two points. The correlation is not established; it is only not yet refuted. Establishing it means repeating the constrained/unconstrained pair on several machines.
  • The unrestricted reference is a lower bound. It started from the planar optimum, so it may be basin-limited, and its resolution-independence rests on a spectral argument rather than a completed convergence run.
Context

Coil count dominates coil shape by roughly an order of magnitude. Every shape lever tested on Helios moved bn by 16–24%. Going from 12 to 16 coils moved it by 120% (8.4220e−3 → 3.7991e−3), closing 95% of the gap from the planar baseline to the published planar-plus-shaping result, versus 82% for 12 coils of any shape.

Read the other way, this is the strongest case for the piecewise-planar concept: the flat-piece restriction is cheap, so the engineering budget is better spent on more coils than on more complicated ones. It also bounds how much a planarity metric can ever be worth — it is screening a second-order effect.
